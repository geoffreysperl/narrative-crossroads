# From the Workshop
{: .no_toc }

If you were in a Quest-Based Learning session, this is your follow-up page. If someone told you about it and you weren't there, everything here works without having attended — you'll just be reading the scenario instead of remembering it.

**If you only do one thing:** go to the [Quickstart]({% link quickstart.md %}) and run it. Everything else on this page is optional depth.

## What Happened in the Room

You played a scenario as an unnamed witness — not a canonical character, but someone standing in the crowd while a text's central moment happened around you. At the threshold, you made a choice with your body: you stood, or you didn't. You pressed, or you didn't.

Then we asked: **"What did you just do?"**

That question is the method. The game exists to produce an experience worth debriefing. The debrief is where the analysis lives.

The three claims from the session, in order:

1. **The card is the stick.** A child with a stick is a knight. A student with a character card is inside the text. The prop does the work of imagination that instruction alone can't assign.
2. **You don't adopt a game. You steal its procedures.** A game is a setting plus a set of procedures. The procedures are what your classroom wants.
3. **The minimum is one card, one scene, one question.** Everything else is elaboration.

## Running the Scenario Yourself

The scenarios used in the session are built on short texts that are still in copyright, so the texts themselves aren't hosted here — you'll need your own classroom copies. The facilitator structure is below.

Both scenarios use the same engine:

- **A clock.** Seven narrated beats, delivered in order, no stopping mid-clock.
- **A physical threshold.** The choice is made with the body, not the mouth. Standing, stepping, pressing, placing a token.
- **A reckoning.** After the choice, before the debrief: the moment the consequence lands.

The difference between the two is the shape of the pressure:

- **Frenzy** (a crowd dissolving an individual) needs a crowd to fire. Powerful, but it fails in a small room.
- **Revelation** (a lucid individual talked into something) works at any size, including one participant.

Pick by your room. If you have fewer than about eight people, run revelation.

## The Seven Procedures

The toolkit segment's premise: strip the setting off a game and keep the procedure underneath.

| Procedure | The classroom job it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Session zero** | Establishing norms students actually own |
| **Community and place** | Building shared context before content |
| **A choice that costs something** | Making stakes real enough to reason about |
| **"What do you do?"** | Converting comprehension into commitment |
| **Safety and calibration** | Giving students a way out that isn't a confession |
| **Structure that survives absence** | Running a unit when a third of the room is gone |
| **Students inventing the rules** | Handing over authorship |

The full menu — with a specific game behind each procedure — isn't posted here. Ask at <geoffrey@geoffreysperl.com> and I'll send it.
